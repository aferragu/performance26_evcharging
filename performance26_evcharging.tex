\begin{abstract}
We study a battery-buffered electric vehicle fast charging station modeled as a
piecewise-deterministic Markov process. A battery mediates between the grid
connection and one or more chargers, enabling fast service when the battery is charged and falling
back to the grid rate when depleted. For the single-server loss system we derive the
stationary distribution in closed form, uncovering a decoupling property whereby the
charger state and battery level are independent in steady state, and obtain explicit
formulas for several performance metrics. We extend the analysis to the
multi-server loss system, showing that the stationary densities retain an exponential
form with occupancy weights governed by a simple recurrence, and that the classical
Erlang-B formula is recovered at the critical load where the mean battery drift vanishes.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{Piecewise deterministic Markov processes, Electrical Vehicles.}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% -------------------------------------------------------

The rapid adoption of electric vehicles (EVs) is placing increasing demands on electrical
infrastructure. A key challenge is the provisioning of public \emph{fast charging}: modern DC
fast chargers can deliver 150~kW or more per port, requiring grid connections sized for
their peak output. Yet in practice a charging station is not used continuously; vehicles
arrive stochastically, and the chargers are idle most of the time. Sizing the grid
connection for worst-case simultaneous demand is therefore wasteful, civil infrastructure becomes costly, and utility demand
charges (fees based on peak power draw) can represent the dominant
operating cost of a fast charging site~\cite{NREL2023}. A battery, acting as an energy buffer, placed between
the grid and the chargers resolves this mismatch: the battery gradually charges from the
grid at a steady, lower rate and discharges at high power during the brief intervals a
vehicle is present, enabling a much smaller and cheaper grid connection. While battery storage
at EV stations has been studied before, it has been done primarily in the context of renewable energy integration,
where the battery smooths intermittent solar or wind supply~\cite{Bayram2011,Kabir2020,Tran2019}. However, with the advent of fast DC chargers available in highways and remote areas, using a battery buffer in these low-utilization regimes has become equally compelling for purely grid-connected stations.

The central analytical question this raises is a queueing one: given that vehicles
arrive randomly and the battery recharges during idle or low utilization periods, how often does a vehicle
arrive to find the battery depleted, forcing service at the slower grid rate? This
coupling between the continuous battery state and the discrete service rate is what
distinguishes the battery-buffered station from a standard queue, and it has not
been captured in the existing EV charging literature, which models charging with a fixed
service rate~\cite{Bayram2013,Dupont2023,Tan2014}.

In this paper, we propose a station model using a Piecewise-Deterministic Markov Process
(PDMP)~\cite{Davis1984,Davis1993}: the battery level evolves deterministically between
random events, while car arrivals and departures trigger instantaneous switches.
The state is then the pair $(n, x)$ of system occupancy and battery level. We derive the generator
of this process, and compute the stationary distribution in closed form for the single-server loss system. 
A key structural finding is a \emph{decoupling property}: in steady state the charger occupancy and battery level
are conditionally independent given the battery is the interior of the state space (partially charged), so that the
stationary density factors as a product of an exponential in $x$ and a mode-dependent
weight. This yields explicit formulas for the blocking probability, mean charging time,
and a \emph{slowdown factor} quantifying the service degradation as a function of a
car's energy demand. We then extend the analysis to a loss system with $s$ parallel
chargers sharing a common battery. The exponential form of the stationary density is
preserved, with the mode weights now governed by a simple recurrence. Armed with these formulas, we discuss several performance issues and design objectives that provide guidelines on how to design such systems.

The paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:model} introduces the single-server
model and its PDMP formulation. Section~\ref{sec:stationary} derives the stationary
distribution and performance metrics. Section~\ref{sec:multiserver} treats the
many-server case. Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} concludes.

\section{Single-Server Model}
\label{sec:model}
% -------------------------------------------------------

We consider a single EV charging station consisting of one charger connected to the
electrical grid, equipped with a battery buffer of capacity $B > 0$. Cars arrive
according to a Poisson process of rate $\lambda > 0$, each carrying an energy demand
$Y \sim \mathrm{Exp}(\mu)$. The station operates as a \emph{loss system}: a car that
arrives when the charger is busy is turned away.

The system parameters are the following: the grid may supply a maximum power $p_1$, and this energy may be used to charge the battery or the car, if present. When the system is idle, the battery is recharged at rate $0<p_0\leqslant p_1$. Inequality reflects that we may want to recharge the battery at a slower rate to preserve battery life. The EV charging station itself may provide power $p_2>p_1$, which amounts to fast charging. When a car is present, this power is provided by the grid at rate $p_1$ and the extra amount $c:=p_2-p_1>0$ is provided by the battery, whenever is not empty. If the battery empties, the system reverts to charging only from the grid at rate $p_1$, and the battery stays depleted until the current car finishes service. We denote by $x(t)\in[0,B]$ the current state of the battery at time $t$, and by $n(t)\in\{0,1\}$ the presence of not of a charging vehicle.

Under the above assumptions, when a car is admitted, service proceeds in at most two phases. In \emph{Phase~1}
(fast charging), while the battery level $x > 0$, the car charges at rate $p_2$ and the
battery discharges at rate $c$. In \emph{Phase~2} (slow charging), once the battery is
exhausted, $x = 0$ and the car charges at rate $p_1$, while $x$ remains at zero. A car with small demand may complete service entirely in Phase~1 if its service requirement is low enough. Under the exponential energy demands assumed before, the instantaneous departure rate from the system is therefore $\mu p(x)$, where:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:power_function}
p(x) = p_2\,\mathbf{1}_{x>0} + p_1\,\mathbf{1}_{x=0}.
\end{equation}
The system state at time $t$ is thus the pair $Z(t) = (n(t), x(t))$. Between arrival and departure events, the battery evolves deterministically according to $\dot{x} = r(n, x)$, where
\begin{equation}
  r(n, x) = \begin{cases}
    p_0  & n = 0,\ x < B, \\
    0    & n = 0,\ x = B, \\
    -c   & n = 1,\ x > 0, \\
    0    & n = 1,\ x = 0.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:vectorfield}
\end{equation}
The jumps are spontaneous events: \emph{arrivals} at $(0,x)$ occur at rate $\lambda$ and
the states transitions (deterministically) to $(1,x)$; \emph{departures} at $(1,x)$ with $x>0$ occur at rate $\mu p_2$
and transition to $(0,x)$; \emph{departures} at $(1,0)$ occur at rate $\mu p_1$ and
transition to $(0,0)$, or in short $(1,x)\mapsto (0,x)$ at rate $\mu p(x)$. In all cases the battery level is unchanged at the jump epoch.

The process $Z(t)$ with state space $\{0,1\}\times[0,B]$ fits the PDMP framework of Davis~\cite{Davis1984,Davis1993},
though the standard theory requires an open state space and forces the process to jump
before reaching the boundary. Neither condition holds here: $E$ is compact, and the flow
reaches $x=0$ and $x=B$ in finite time without a jump. Nevertheless, the vector
field~\eqref{eq:vectorfield} is bounded and monotone on each mode, so the flow is
globally well-defined. The strong Markov property follows from the martingale-problem
approach, which extends to this setting since all jump rates are bounded and the jump
kernel leaves $x$ unchanged, i.e. jumps in the continuous variable are bounded.

The extended generator $\mathcal{A}$ of $Z(t)$ has domain $\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})$
consisting of functions $\psi : E \to \mathbb{R}$ such that $\psi(n,\cdot)$ is absolutely
continuous on $[0,B]$ for each $n$. For $\psi \in \mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})$,
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:generator}
\begin{align}
  \mathcal{A}\psi(0, x) &= r(0,x)\,\psi_0'(x) + \lambda\,[\psi(1,x) - \psi(0,x)],
  \label{eq:gen0} \\
  \mathcal{A}\psi(1, x) &= r(1,x)\,\psi_1'(x) + \mu p(x)\,[\psi(0,x) - \psi(1,x)],
  \label{eq:gen1}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $\psi_n' = \partial_x \psi(n,\cdot)$ is defined Lebesgue-a.e. The first term in
each line is the flow contribution; the second is the jump contribution, which in fact just switches mass from $n=0$ to $n=1$ and viceversa leaving $x$ unchanged. For any
$\psi \in \mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})$, the process $M_t^\psi := \psi(Z(t)) - \psi(Z(0)) - \int_0^t \mathcal{A}\psi(Z(s))\,ds$
is a local martingale~\cite{Davis1984}, and the process is right-continuous and a strong Markov process.

Note that an arriving car always finds $x>0$ with probability one. During any idle period
the battery charges at rate $p_0 > 0$, and since the idle time before the next arrival is
$\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)$ (positive a.s.), the battery is strictly positive at every arrival
epoch. The state $(n=0, x=0)$ therefore should have zero probability under any stationary
distribution.

The adjoint operator $\mathcal{A}^*$ operates on the probability distributions on $E$ and provides the forward Kolmogorov equation of the system \cite{Davis1993}. A stationary distribution $\pi$ satisfies $\int \mathcal{A}\psi\,d\pi = 0$ for all
$\psi \in \mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})$, which is the weak form of the Kolmogorov forward
equation $\mathcal{A}^*\pi = 0$. We now proceed to solve the steady state of this system by positing a particular form of $\pi$ using the characteristics of the system.

\section{Single-Server in steady-state}
\label{sec:stationary}
% -------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Stationary distribution}\label{ssec:stationary_dist}

Since $E$ is compact and $Z(t)$ is irreducible (every open rectangle
$\{n\}\times(a,b)$ is reachable with positive probability from any starting point, by
a combination of deterministic drift and Poisson events), the process is ergodic with a
unique stationary distribution $\pi$~\cite{MeynTweedie1993}.

We now seek $\pi$ as a mixed measure with densities on $(0,B)$ and point masses at the boundary points where flow accumulates. Therefore, we propose the following solution structure:
\begin{equation}
  \pi = f_0(x)\,dx + f_1(x)\,dx + \pi_{0,B}\,\delta_{(0,B)} + \pi_{1,0}\,\delta_{(1,0)}. 
  \label{eq:ansatz}
\end{equation}
Here $f_n(x)$ $n=0,1$ are the interior densities and $\pi_{0,B}, \pi_{1,0} \geq 0$ are point
masses at the corners where the flow accumulates: $x=B$ in mode~$0$ (since $r(0,\cdot)=p_0>0$)
and $x=0$ in mode~$1$ (since $r(1,\cdot)=-c<0$). The other two corners should carry no atoms since flow arriving on them is immediately carried into the interior by the flow (cf. Figure \ref{fig:dynamics_single_server})

Substituting this form into $\int \mathcal{A}\psi\,d\pi = 0$ and using eqs. \eqref{eq:generator}, the
stationarity condition $\int \mathcal{A}\psi\,d\pi = 0$ for all $\psi \in \mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})$ yields the following balance equations for the interior densities:
\begin{subequations}
  \begin{align}
  p_0\, f_0'(x) &= -\lambda\, f_0(x) + \mu p_2\, f_1(x), \label{eq:E0} \\
  -c\, f_1'(x)  &= \phantom{-}\lambda\, f_0(x) - \mu p_2\, f_1(x). \label{eq:E1}
  \end{align}

As for the atoms, we obtain the following \emph{boundary flux equations}, obtained by flux conservation at each boundary
point. At $x=0$ in mode $1$, the inward flow $c\,f_1(0^+)$ feeds the atom $\pi_{1,0}$, which
drains via departures at rate $\mu p_1$:
\begin{equation}
  c\,f_1(0^+) = \mu p_1\,\pi_{1,0}. \label{eq:B1}
\end{equation}
Similarly, at $x=B$ in mode $0$, the inward flow $p_0\,f_0(B^-)$ feeds the atom $\pi_{0,B}$,
which drains via arrivals at rate $\lambda$:
\begin{equation}
  p_0\,f_0(B^-) = \lambda\,\pi_{0,B}. \label{eq:B2}
\end{equation}
Finally, at $(n,x)=(0,0)$, mass arriving from departures at rate $\mu p_1$ must immediately be carried at rate $p_0$ into the interior (no atom), yielding:
\begin{equation}
  p_0\,f_0(0^+) = \mu p_1\,\pi_{1,0}. \label{eq:B0}
\end{equation}
And finally by a similar argument, at $(n,x)=(1,B)$
\begin{equation}
  c\,f_1(B^-) = \lambda\,\pi_{0,B}. \label{eq:B3}
\end{equation}
\end{subequations}
Finally we have the normalization condition $\int_0^B (f_0 + f_1)\,dx + \pi_{0,B} + \pi_{1,0} = 1$. We can now state the main result of this section.

\begin{theorem} \label{thm:stationary}
Let $\theta := \mu p_2/c - \lambda/p_0$. The unique stationary distribution of $Z(t)$ has the form~\eqref{eq:ansatz} with
\begin{subequations}
\begin{equation}
  f_0(x) = \frac{e^{\theta x}}{K}, \qquad
  f_1(x) = \frac{p_0}{c}\cdot\frac{e^{\theta x}}{K}, \qquad x \in (0,B),
  \label{eq:densities}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \pi_{1,0} = \frac{p_0}{\mu p_1\, K}, \qquad
  \pi_{0,B} = \frac{p_0\,e^{\theta B}}{\lambda\, K},
  \label{eq:atoms}
\end{equation}
\end{subequations}
and the normalization constant is
\begin{equation}
  K = \frac{p_0+c}{c}\cdot\frac{e^{\theta B}-1}{\theta}
    + \frac{p_0}{\mu p_1} + \frac{p_0\,e^{\theta B}}{\lambda},
  \label{eq:K}
\end{equation}
with the convention $(e^{\theta B}-1)/\theta = B$ when $\theta = 0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[sketch]
Adding \eqref{eq:E0}, \eqref{eq:E1} gives $\frac{d}{dx}[p_0 f_0 - c f_1] = 0$, so $p_0 f_0 - c f_1$ is constant on $(0,B)$. Equations \eqref{eq:B1} and \eqref{eq:B0} together give $p_0 f_0(0^+) = c f_1(0^+)$, so the constant is zero. Therefore the densities should be proportional to each other, i.e. $f_1(x) = (p_0/c)\,f_0(x)$ for all $x \in (0,B)$. Substituting this condition into \eqref{eq:E0} yields a first order differential equation for $f_0$, which accounting for the definition of $\theta$ is simply $f_0'(x) = \theta\, f_0(x)$. We conclude that $f_0$ (and thus also $f_1$) should be exponential: $f_0(x) = f_0(0^+)\,e^{\theta x}$. With this expression, the atoms follow directly from \eqref{eq:B2}, \eqref{eq:B0}, and \eqref{eq:B3} is automatically satisfied, confirming consistency. Setting $f_0(0^+) = 1/K$ and imposing normalization
determines $K$ as in \eqref{eq:K}.
\end{proof}

The parameter $\theta$ has a physical interpretation: define the offered load of the system $\rho:=\lambda/\mu$, i.e. the average external power request. Consider now a single idle-busy cycle in the absence of battery boundaries: the battery
charges at rate $p_0$ during the idle period (mean duration $1/\lambda$) and discharges
at rate $c$ during the busy period with mean duration $1/(\mu p_2)$. Then, the expected net
battery change per cycle is therefore $\Delta := \frac{p_0}{\lambda} - \frac{c}{\mu p_2}$. It is easy to see that $\theta$ has the same sign as $\Delta$, in particular:
\begin{equation*}
  \theta > 0 \Leftrightarrow \rho < \frac{p_0p_2}{c} = \rho \frac{p_0 p_2}{p_2-p_1}
\end{equation*}
This corresponds to a \emph{lightly loaded} station, where the battery gains energy on average on each cycle and thus tends to fill, concentrating its mass towards $x=B$. When $\lambda/\mu > p_0 p_2/c$, we are in the \emph{highly loaded} regime, $\theta < 0$, the battery tends to drain and mass concentrates near $x = 0$, where a significant fraction of service occurs at the slow
rate $p_1$. When $\theta = 0$ the battery neither drains nor fills on average and stationary density is uniform on $(0, B)$.

\subsection{Performance metrics}\label{ssec:metrics}

We now derive the key performance metrics directly from Theorem~\ref{thm:stationary}. We begin by computing the blocking probability, that due to the PASTA property, is just the stationary probability of being in state $1$, i.e.
\begin{equation}
  P_B = \int_0^B f_1(x)\,dx + \pi_{1,0}
      = \frac{p_0}{K}\left(\frac{e^{\theta B}-1}{c\,\theta} + \frac{1}{\mu p_1}\right).
  \label{eq:PB}
\end{equation}
Note that this probability is also the probability that the charger is working. As $B \to 0$, $P_B \to \rho/(\rho + p_1)$, the blocking probability of an $M/M/1/1$ queue at rate $p_1$, since cars can only be served from the grid. Conversely, if the battery is large, as $B \to \infty$ we distinguish two cases: in the lightly loaded regime, $\theta > 0$ and $P_B \to \rho/(\rho + p_2)$, corresponding to service always at the fast rate $p_2$ because the battery can cope with the load and the system operates most of the time in fast charging. However, if the system is highly loaded ($\theta < 0$), $P_B$ converges to a value strictly between these two limits even as $B \to \infty$, reflecting that the system operates \emph{partly in both charging regimes} because the battery regularly depletes.

We now focus on the mean service time. By Little's law, since there is only one server working and arrivals onto a busy system are blocked, we have $E[N] = P_B = \lambda (1-P_B)E[T]$ and therefore, the mean service time of an admitted car is:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}[T] = \frac{P_B}{\lambda(1-P_B)}.
  \label{eq:ET}
\end{equation}
A more meaningful quantity is the \emph{average slowdown}, which we define as $E[T]$ normalized by the average service time in a system that operates at the fast rate $p_2$ permanently, which is given by:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:avg_slowdown}
  \bar{S} := \frac{E[T]}{E[Y]} = \frac{\mu p_2  P_B}{\lambda(1-P_B)} = \frac{p_2}{\rho} \frac{P_B}{1-P_B}
\end{equation}

A final quantity of interest is how different demands are affected in terms of charging time. One expects that larger demands are more affected since the battery may deplete during serive. To this end, define the \emph{slowdown factor}:
\begin{equation}
  S(y) := \frac{\mathbb{E}[T \mid Y=y]}{(y/p_2)}.
\end{equation}
The following proposition is proved in the Appendix.
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:slowdown}
Define the energy threshold $x^* = p_2 B/c$. If $Y=y<x^*$, the battery may be active during the whole charge and: 
\begin{subequations}
  \begin{equation}
  S(y) = 1 + \frac{p_2-p_1}{p_1\,y\,K(1-P_B)\,\theta}
         \left[\frac{p_2}{c\,\theta}\!\left(e^{\theta cy/p_2}-1\right) - y\right].
  \label{eq:Sy1}
\end{equation}
For $y\geqslant x^*$, the battery always depletes during service and:
\begin{multline}
  S(y) = 1 + \frac{p_2-p_1}{p_1\,y\,K(1-P_B)}
         \Bigl\{\tfrac{1}{\theta}\bigl[(y-\tfrac{p_2 B}{c})e^{\theta B} - y
         + \tfrac{p_2(e^{\theta B}-1)}{c\theta}\bigr] \\
         + \tfrac{p_0 e^{\theta B}}{\lambda}(y-\tfrac{p_2 B}{c})\Bigr\}.
  \label{eq:Sy2}
\end{multline}
\end{subequations}
\end{proposition}


\subsection{Optimal battery regime}\label{ssec:optimal_regime}

For practical battery health it is desirable to charge the battery as slowly as
possible and to keep it cycling continuously rather than dwelling near the boundaries
$x=0$ or $x=B$. The stationary distribution of Theorem~\ref{thm:stationary} reveals
a natural design criterion that achieves both goals simultaneously.

\textbf{Design parameters.}
We introduce two dimensionless parameters that will govern the analysis. The
\emph{speed-up ratio} $\alpha = p_2/p_1 > 1$ measures how much faster the charger
operates with a full battery than without. The \emph{normalised battery capacity}
$\beta = \mu B p_2 / c$ is the ratio of the battery drain time $B/c$ to the mean
fast-charge session $1/(\mu p_2)$: it counts how many full fast-charge sessions
the battery can sustain before depleting. Finally, the \emph{normalised load}
$\tilde\rho = \rho/p_2 = \lambda/(\mu p_2)$ is the offered load measured in units
of fast-charge capacity; it is the mean number of fast-charge sessions arriving per
unit of service capacity at rate $p_2$.

\textbf{Optimal recharge rate and feasibility.}
From the exponential form $f_0(x) \propto e^{\theta x}$, mass concentrates near
$x=B$ when $\theta>0$ and near $x=0$ when $\theta<0$; in either case the battery
spends time pinned at a boundary. The uniform regime $\theta=0$ eliminates this,
giving a flat stationary density on $(0,B)$. Since $\theta = \mu p_2/c - \lambda/p_0$,
setting $\theta=0$ uniquely determines the idle recharge rate:
\begin{equation}
  p_0^* = \tilde\rho\, c.
  \label{eq:p0star}
\end{equation}
This is also the \emph{slowest} recharge rate achieving $\theta=0$, which minimises
battery stress. The constraint $p_0^* \leq p_1$ requires
\begin{equation}
  \tilde\rho \leq \frac{p_1}{c} = \frac{1}{\alpha - 1},
  \qquad \text{equivalently} \qquad
  \rho \leq \rho_{\max} = \frac{p_1 p_2}{c}.
  \label{eq:rhomax}
\end{equation}
Below this threshold the operator can always achieve the uniform regime by tuning
$p_0$; above it, $\theta < 0$ regardless of $p_0$.

\textbf{Average slowdown.}
Setting $\theta=0$ and $p_0 = p_0^*$ in the expressions for $K$ and $P_B$, one finds
that $K$ cancels in the ratio $P_B/(1-P_B)$ and the load $\rho$ drops out entirely,
leaving:
\begin{equation}
  \bar{S} = \frac{\beta + \alpha}{\beta + 1}.
  \label{eq:Sbar_beta}
\end{equation}
This depends only on $\alpha$ and $\beta$, not on the load. It is a useful design
property: the average slowdown is determined entirely by hardware parameters, and
is the same for any load $\tilde\rho \leq 1/(\alpha-1)$. As $\beta \to 0$,
$\bar{S} \to \alpha = p_2/p_1$ (no battery, all service at the slow rate); as
$\beta \to \infty$, $\bar{S} \to 1$ (infinite battery, always fast). The battery
capacity achieving a target $\bar{S}_0 \in (1,\alpha)$ is:
\begin{equation}
  \beta^* = \frac{\alpha - \bar{S}_0}{\bar{S}_0 - 1}
  \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad
  B^* = \frac{c}{\mu p_2}\cdot\frac{\alpha - \bar{S}_0}{\bar{S}_0 - 1}.
  \label{eq:Bstar}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Blocking probability.}
Substituting $\theta=0$ and $p_0=p_0^*$ into the expression for $P_B$ in
Proposition~\ref{prop:blocking}, the normalization constant $K$ cancels and one obtains:
\begin{equation}
  P_B = \frac{\tilde\rho(\beta+\alpha)}{\beta + 1 + \tilde\rho(\beta+\alpha)}.
  \label{eq:PB_opt}
\end{equation}
This has the form of an M/M/1/1 blocking formula with effective load
$\tilde\rho\bar{S}$, since $P_B = \tilde\rho\bar{S}/(1+\tilde\rho\bar{S})$.
Unlike $\bar{S}$, the blocking probability depends on $\tilde\rho$, as expected.

% -------------------------------------------------------
\section{The Many-Server Case}
\label{sec:multiserver}
% -------------------------------------------------------

We now extend the model to $s \geq 1$ parallel chargers sharing a single battery buffer
of capacity $B$ and a single grid connection of rate $p_1$. The station continues to
operate as a loss system: a car that arrives when all $s$ chargers are busy is turned away.

\textbf{Assumptions.} We impose $p_1 < s p_2$, so the grid alone cannot sustain all $s$
chargers simultaneously at full rate; the battery is essential at full occupancy. We
also set $p_0 = p_1$, so the idle recharge rate equals the grid supply rate;
this simplifies the structure of the vector field.

\textbf{State space and dynamics.} The state is $Z(t)=(n(t),x(t))$ with
$n(t)\in\{0,\ldots,s\}$ busy chargers and $x(t)\in[0,B]$;
$E=\{0,\ldots,s\}\times[0,B]$ is compact. With $n$ busy chargers each drawing
$p_2$ and the grid supplying $p_1$, the net drift is $r_n = p_1-np_2$.
Setting $p_0=p_1$ makes this hold for all $n$: the battery charges at rate
$p_1$ when idle and drains at rate $np_2-p_1$ when $n>p_1/p_2$. The vector field is
\begin{equation}
  \dot{x} = \begin{cases}
    r_n & 0 < x < B,\ r_n \neq 0, \\
    0   & \text{otherwise (boundary reflection).}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:vf_multi}
\end{equation}
Define the \emph{critical occupancy} $n^* = \lfloor p_1/p_2 \rfloor \in \{0,\ldots,s-1\}$;
the battery charges when $n \leq n^*$ and drains when $n > n^*$.

The jumps are: arrivals at rate $\lambda$ when $n < s$, transitioning $n \to n+1$ with
$x$ unchanged; departures at total rate $n\mu p_2$ when $x > 0$, and at rate $\mu p_1$
when $x = 0$ (the grid supplies $p_1$ shared equally, giving total throughput $\mu p_1$
regardless of occupancy), each transitioning $n \to n-1$ with $x$ unchanged.

The process $Z(t)$ is again a PDMP on a compact state space, and the same
martingale-problem argument as in Section~\ref{sec:model} establishes the strong Markov
property. Irreducibility follows by the same reachability argument, so $Z(t)$ is ergodic
with a unique stationary distribution.

\textbf{Form of the stationary distribution.} The flow direction at each boundary point
now depends on the occupancy level $n$. Atoms accumulate at $(n, B)$ for $n \leq n^*$
(where the flow pushes toward $x = B$) and at $(n, 0)$ for $n > n^*$ (where the flow
pushes toward $x = 0$). We therefore seek a stationary distribution of the form
\begin{equation}
  \pi = \sum_{n=0}^{s} f_n(x)\,dx
      + \sum_{n=0}^{n^*} \pi_n^{(B)}\,\delta_{(n,B)}
      + \sum_{n=n^*+1}^{s} \pi_n^{(0)}\,\delta_{(n,0)},
  \label{eq:form_multi}
\end{equation}
where $f_n \geq 0$ are densities on $(0,B)$ and $\pi_n^{(B)}, \pi_n^{(0)} \geq 0$ are
point masses.

\textbf{Interior equations.} Setting $\mathcal{A}^*\pi = 0$ on $(0,B)$ gives, for
$0 \leq n \leq s$,
\begin{multline}
  r_n f_n'(x) = \lambda f_{n-1}(x)\,\mathbf{1}_{n>0}
              + (n{+}1)\mu p_2\, f_{n+1}(x)\,\mathbf{1}_{n<s} \\
              - (\lambda\,\mathbf{1}_{n<s} + n\mu p_2)\, f_n(x).
  \label{eq:En_multi}
\end{multline}

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:multi}
Let $\rho = \lambda/(\mu p_2)$. There exists a unique $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$, determined
by the boundary flux equations together with normalization, such that the stationary
densities take the form
\begin{equation}
  f_n(x) = C\,a_n(\theta)\,e^{\theta x}, \qquad x \in (0,B),\ n = 0,\ldots,s,
  \label{eq:fn_multi}
\end{equation}
where $C > 0$ is a normalization constant and the coefficients $a_n(\theta)$ satisfy
the recurrence $a_0 = 1$ and
\begin{equation}
  a_{n+1} = \frac{\theta\,S_n + \lambda\,a_n}{(n+1)\mu p_2},
  \qquad S_n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} r_k\,a_k, \quad n = 0,\ldots,s-1.
  \label{eq:recurrence}
\end{equation}
When $\theta = 0$, the recurrence yields $a_n = \rho^n/n!$ and the distribution reduces
to the classical Erlang-B form with uniform density in $x$. For $\theta \neq 0$, the
blocking probability
\begin{equation}
  P_B = C\,a_s\,\frac{e^{\theta B}-1}{\theta} + \pi_s^{(0)}\,\mathbf{1}_{n^* < s}
  \label{eq:PB_multi}
\end{equation}
deviates from the Erlang-B value, with the battery shifting mass toward $x = B$ when
$\theta > 0$ and toward $x = 0$ when $\theta < 0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[sketch]
Summing~\eqref{eq:En_multi} from $k=0$ to $n$ telescopes the right side to
$-\lambda f_n + (n+1)\mu p_2 f_{n+1}$. The exponential form $f_n = Ca_n e^{\theta x}$
then yields~\eqref{eq:recurrence}. The value of $\theta$ is fixed by the boundary flux
equations together with normalization. For $s=1$ and $p_0=p_1$, one recovers
$\theta = \mu p_2/c - \lambda/p_1$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:stationary}.
\end{proof}

The parameter $\theta$ admits the same physical interpretation as in the single-server
case. In the unconstrained system (infinite battery), the occupancy evolves as an
$M/M/s/s$ queue with Erlang-B stationary weights $\pi_n \propto \rho^n/n!$, giving a
mean battery drift $\bar{r} = p_1 - (\lambda/\mu)(1 - P_B^{\text{Erlang}})$. The sign
of $\bar{r}$ determines the sign of $\theta$: positive when the grid can sustain the
average load (battery fills in the long run) and negative otherwise. The case $\theta = 0$
corresponds exactly to $\bar{r} = 0$, at which point the Erlang-B distribution is recovered.

\textbf{Performance metrics.} The blocking probability is given by~\eqref{eq:PB_multi}.
By PASTA and Little's law the mean charging time follows from $P_B$ exactly as in
Propositions~\ref{prop:blocking} and~\ref{prop:meantime}. The slowdown factor
$S(y)$ generalizes directly: the admitted battery level has the same exponential density
$f_0(x)/({1-P_B})$ on $(0,B)$ (now with $s$-server normalization), and the conditional
charging time $T(x,y)$ is unchanged, so the formulas of
Proposition~\ref{prop:slowdown} apply with the updated $P_B$ and $K = 1/C$.

% -------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
% -------------------------------------------------------

We have studied a battery-buffered EV fast charging station using the framework of
piecewise-deterministic Markov processes. For the single-server loss system we derived
the stationary distribution in closed form, establishing a decoupling property that
separates the battery level from the charger state in steady state, and obtained explicit
expressions for the blocking probability, mean charging time, and slowdown factor. We
extended the analysis to a loss system with $s$ parallel chargers sharing a common
battery, showing that the stationary densities retain an exponential form in the battery
level with occupancy-dependent weights governed by a simple recurrence. In both cases the
key parameter $\theta$, the sign of the mean battery drift, determines whether the system
operates in a battery-rich or battery-scarce regime and controls the deviation of the
blocking probability from the classical Erlang-B value.

The analysis highlights a structural difference from standard fluid queues: the
state-dependent service rate at $x = 0$ couples the discrete and continuous components
in a way that prevents a product-form solution in the many-server case while still
admitting an exponential form in $x$. Numerical illustrations of the performance metrics
and a study of the optimal battery sizing problem are left for future work.

\section{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:slowdown}}

\textbf{Charging time formula.}
Given admission when the battery is at level $x \in (0,B)$ and the car has demand
$Y = y$, service proceeds in two phases. In Phase~1 (fast charging at rate $p_2$),
the battery discharges at rate $c$, so it is exhausted after delivering energy
$p_2 \cdot (x/c) = p_2 x/c$ to the car; this takes time $x/c$. If $y \leq p_2 x/c$,
the car completes in Phase~1 with charging time $y/p_2$. Otherwise, the remaining
demand $y - p_2 x/c$ is served in Phase~2 at rate $p_1$, taking additional time
$(y - p_2 x/c)/p_1$. Hence the total charging time is
\begin{equation}
  T(x,y) = \frac{y}{p_2} + \left(\frac{1}{p_1} - \frac{1}{p_2}\right)
            \max\!\left(y - \frac{p_2 x}{c},\, 0\right). \label{eq:Txy}
\end{equation}
The slowdown factor $S(y) = \mathbb{E}[T \mid Y=y]\,/\,(y/p_2)$ measures the ratio of
actual to ideal (all-fast) charging time.

\textbf{Averaging over the admitted battery distribution.}
By PASTA, an admitted car sees the stationary distribution conditioned on $n=0$.
The admitted battery level has density $g(x) = f_0(x)/(1-P_B)$ on $(0,B)$, plus
atom $\pi_{0,B}/(1-P_B)$ at $x = B$. Substituting \eqref{eq:Txy} and writing
$x^* = cy/p_2$ for the critical level at which the battery would be exactly depleted:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}[T \mid Y=y] = \frac{y}{p_2} + \frac{p_2 - p_1}{p_1 p_2}
  \cdot \frac{1}{1-P_B}
  \int_0^{\min(x^*,\,B)} \!\left(y - \frac{p_2 x}{c}\right) f_0(x)\,dx
  + \mathbf{1}_{y \geq p_2B/c}\,\frac{\pi_{0,B}}{1-P_B}\left(y - \frac{p_2 B}{c}\right)
  \cdot\frac{p_2-p_1}{p_1 p_2}.
  \label{eq:ET_y}
\end{equation}
The indicator term accounts for cars admitted from the atom at $x=B$ when their demand
exceeds $p_2 B/c$, in which case the battery is always depleted during service.

\textbf{Case 1: $y < x^* = p_2B/c$.}
Here $\min(x^*,B)=x^*$ and no atom contribution arises. Using $f_0(x) = e^{\theta x}/K$:
\begin{align*}
  \int_0^{x^*} \left(y - \frac{p_2 x}{c}\right) \frac{e^{\theta x}}{K}\,dx
  &= \frac{1}{K}\left[y\,\frac{e^{\theta x^*}-1}{\theta}
     - \frac{p_2}{c}\int_0^{x^*} x\,e^{\theta x}\,dx\right] \\
  &= \frac{1}{K}\left[y\,\frac{e^{\theta x^*}-1}{\theta}
     - \frac{p_2}{c}\left(\frac{x^* e^{\theta x^*}}{\theta}
     - \frac{e^{\theta x^*}-1}{\theta^2}\right)\right].
\end{align*}
Substituting $x^* = cy/p_2$ and simplifying yields \eqref{eq:Sy1}.

\textbf{Case 2: $y \geq x^* = p_2B/c$.}
Here the battery is always depleted during service regardless of the admission level.
Setting $\min(x^*,B) = B$:
\begin{align*}
  \int_0^{B} \left(y - \frac{p_2 x}{c}\right) \frac{e^{\theta x}}{K}\,dx
  &= \frac{1}{K}\left[y\,\frac{e^{\theta B}-1}{\theta}
     - \frac{p_2}{c}\left(\frac{B e^{\theta B}}{\theta}
     - \frac{e^{\theta B}-1}{\theta^2}\right)\right].
\end{align*}
Adding the atom contribution $({\pi_{0,B}}/{K(1-P_B)})(y - p_2 B/c)$ with
$\pi_{0,B} = p_0 e^{\theta B}/(\lambda K)$ and collecting terms yields \eqref{eq:Sy2}.

\textbf{Monotonicity and limiting behaviour.}
Since $(1/p_1 - 1/p_2) > 0$ and the Phase~2 component $\max(y - p_2 x/c, 0)$ is
non-decreasing in $y$, we have $S(y) \geq 1$ for all $y$, with $S(y) \to 1$ as
$y \to 0$ (all service in Phase~1). As $y \to \infty$ the Phase~2 component dominates
and $S(y) \to p_2/p_1$. As $B \to \infty$ the battery is never depleted and
$S(y) \to 1$; as $B \to 0$ all service is at rate $p_1$ and $S(y) = p_2/p_1$. \qed
