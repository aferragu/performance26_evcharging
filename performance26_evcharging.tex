\begin{abstract}
We study a battery-buffered electric vehicle fast charging station modeled as a
piecewise-deterministic Markov process. A battery mediates between the grid
connection and one or more chargers, enabling fast service when the battery is charged and falling
back to the grid rate when depleted. For the single-server loss system we derive the
stationary distribution in closed form, uncovering a decoupling property whereby the
charger state and battery level are independent in steady state, and obtain explicit
formulas for several performance metrics. We extend the analysis to the
multi-server loss system, showing that the stationary densities retain an exponential
form with occupancy weights governed by a simple recurrence, and that the classical
Erlang-B formula is recovered at the critical load where the mean battery drift vanishes.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{Piecewise deterministic Markov processes, Electrical Vehicles.}

% -------------------------------------------------------
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% -------------------------------------------------------

The rapid adoption of electric vehicles (EVs) is placing increasing demands on electrical
infrastructure. A key challenge is the provisioning of public \emph{fast charging}: modern DC
fast chargers can deliver 150~kW or more per port, requiring grid connections sized for
their peak output. Yet in practice a charging station is not used continuously; vehicles
arrive stochastically, and the chargers are idle most of the time. Sizing the grid
connection for worst-case simultaneous demand is therefore wasteful, civil infrastructure becomes costly, and utility demand
charges (fees based on peak power draw) can represent the dominant
operating cost of a fast charging site~\cite{NREL2023}. A battery, acting as an energy buffer, placed between
the grid and the chargers resolves this mismatch: the battery gradually charges from the
grid at a steady, lower rate and discharges at high power during the brief intervals a
vehicle is present, enabling a much smaller and cheaper grid connection. While battery storage
at EV stations has been studied before, it has been done primarily in the context of renewable energy integration,
where the battery smooths intermittent solar or wind supply~\cite{Bayram2011,Kabir2020,Tran2019}. However, with the advent of fast DC chargers available in highways and remote areas, using a battery buffer in these low-utilization regimes has become equally compelling for purely grid-connected stations.

The central analytical question this raises is a queueing one: given that vehicles
arrive randomly and the battery recharges during idle or low utilization periods, how often does a vehicle
arrive to find the battery depleted, forcing service at the slower grid rate? This
coupling between the continuous battery state and the discrete service rate is what
distinguishes the battery-buffered station from a standard queue, and it has not
been captured in the existing EV charging literature, which models charging with a fixed
service rate~\cite{Bayram2013,Dupont2023,Tan2014}.

In this paper, we propose a station model using a Piecewise-Deterministic Markov Process
(PDMP)~\cite{Davis1984,Davis1993}: the battery level evolves deterministically between
random events, while car arrivals and departures trigger instantaneous switches.
The state is then the pair $(n, x)$ of system occupancy and battery level. We derive the generator
of this process, and compute the stationary distribution in closed form for the single-server loss system. 
A key structural finding is a \emph{decoupling property}: in steady state the charger occupancy and battery level
are conditionally independent given the battery is the interior of the state space (partially charged), so that the
stationary density factors as a product of an exponential in $x$ and a mode-dependent
weight. This yields explicit formulas for the blocking probability, mean charging time,
and a \emph{slowdown factor} quantifying the service degradation as a function of a
car's energy demand. We then extend the analysis to a loss system with $s$ parallel
chargers sharing a common battery. The exponential form of the stationary density is
preserved, with the mode weights now governed by a simple recurrence. Armed with these formulas, we discuss several performance issues and design objectives that provide guidelines on how to design such systems.

The paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:model} introduces the single-server
model and its PDMP formulation. Section~\ref{sec:stationary} derives the stationary
distribution and performance metrics. Section~\ref{sec:multiserver} treats the
many-server case. Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} concludes.




% -------------------------------------------------------
\section{Single-Server Model}
\label{sec:model}
% -------------------------------------------------------

We consider a single EV charging station consisting of one charger connected to the
electrical grid, equipped with a battery buffer of capacity $B > 0$. Cars arrive
according to a Poisson process of rate $\lambda > 0$, each carrying an energy demand
$Y \sim \mathrm{Exp}(\mu)$. The station operates as a \emph{loss system}: a car that
arrives when the charger is busy is turned away.

The system is characterized by the following power rates: $p_0 > 0$, the rate at which
the grid recharges the battery when the charger is idle; $p_1 > p_0$, the maximum grid
supply rate; $p_2 > p_1$, the fast charging rate delivered to a car when the battery is
positive; and $c = p_2 - p_1 > 0$, the net battery discharge rate during fast charging.
The ordering $p_0 \leq p_1 < p_2$ reflects a practical constraint: the battery recharges
slowly to extend its life, while fast charging draws power simultaneously from both grid
and battery.

When a car is admitted, service proceeds in at most two phases. In \emph{Phase~1}
(fast charging), while the battery level $x > 0$, the car charges at rate $p_2$ and the
battery discharges at rate $c$. In \emph{Phase~2} (slow charging), once the battery is
exhausted ($x = 0$), the car charges at rate $p_1$ and the battery remains at zero. A car with small demand may complete service entirely in Phase~1. With exponential demands,
the instantaneous departure rate is $\mu p(x)$, where
$p(x) = p_2\,\mathbf{1}_{x>0} + p_1\,\mathbf{1}_{x=0}$.

The system state at time $t$ is the pair $Z(t) = (n(t), x(t))$, where $n(t) \in \{0,1\}$
is the charger status ($0$ = idle, $1$ = busy) and $x(t) \in [0,B]$ is the battery
level. The state space is $E = \{0,1\} \times [0,B]$. Between events, the battery evolves
deterministically according to $\dot{x} = r(n, x)$, where
\begin{equation}
  r(n, x) = \begin{cases}
    p_0  & n = 0,\ x < B, \\
    0    & n = 0,\ x = B, \\
    -c   & n = 1,\ x > 0, \\
    0    & n = 1,\ x = 0.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:vectorfield}
\end{equation}
The jumps are spontaneous events: \emph{arrivals} at $(0,x)$ occur at rate $\lambda$ and
transition to $(1,x)$; \emph{departures} at $(1,x)$ with $x>0$ occur at rate $\mu p_2$
and transition to $(0,x)$; \emph{departures} at $(1,0)$ occur at rate $\mu p_1$ and
transition to $(0,0)$. In all cases the battery level is unchanged at the jump epoch.

The process $Z(t)$ fits the PDMP framework of Davis~\cite{Davis1984,Davis1993},
though the standard theory requires an open state space and forces the process to jump
before reaching the boundary. Neither condition holds here: $E$ is compact and the flow
reaches $x=0$ and $x=B$ in finite time without a jump. Nevertheless, the vector
field~\eqref{eq:vectorfield} is bounded and monotone on each mode, so the flow is
globally well-defined. The strong Markov property follows from the martingale-problem
approach, which extends to this setting since all jump rates are bounded and the jump
kernel leaves $x$ unchanged.

Note that an arriving car always finds $x>0$ with probability one. During any idle period
the battery charges at rate $p_0 > 0$, and since the idle time before the next arrival is
$\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)$ (positive a.s.), the battery is strictly positive at every arrival
epoch. The state $(n=0, x=0)$ therefore has zero probability under any stationary
distribution.

The extended generator $\mathcal{A}$ of $Z(t)$ has domain $\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})$
consisting of functions $\psi : E \to \mathbb{R}$ such that $\psi(n,\cdot)$ is absolutely
continuous on $[0,B]$ for each $n$. For $\psi \in \mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})$,
\begin{align}
  \mathcal{A}\psi(0, x) &= r(0,x)\,\psi_0'(x) + \lambda\,[\psi(1,x) - \psi(0,x)],
  \label{eq:gen0} \\
  \mathcal{A}\psi(1, x) &= r(1,x)\,\psi_1'(x) + \mu p(x)\,[\psi(0,x) - \psi(1,x)],
  \label{eq:gen1}
\end{align}
where $\psi_n' = \partial_x \psi(n,\cdot)$ is defined Lebesgue-a.e. The first term in
each line is the flow contribution; the second is the jump contribution. For any
$\psi \in \mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})$, the process $M_t^\psi := \psi(Z(t)) - \psi(Z(0)) - \int_0^t \mathcal{A}\psi(Z(s))\,ds$
is a local martingale~\cite{Davis1984}.

A stationary distribution $\pi$ satisfies $\int \mathcal{A}\psi\,d\pi = 0$ for all
$\psi \in \mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})$, which is the weak form of the Kolmogorov forward
equation $\mathcal{A}^*\pi = 0$. We seek $\pi$ as a mixed measure with densities on
$(0,B)$ and point masses at the boundary points where flow accumulates. Substituting
this form into $\int \mathcal{A}\psi\,d\pi = 0$ and integrating by parts yields a
system of ODEs on $(0,B)$ and flux-balance conditions at the boundary, which we solve
in the next section.

% -------------------------------------------------------
\section{Single-Server Steady-State Analysis}
\label{sec:stationary}
% -------------------------------------------------------

Since $E$ is compact and $Z(t)$ is irreducible (every open rectangle
$\{n\}\times(a,b)$ is reachable with positive probability from any starting point, by
a combination of deterministic drift and Poisson events), the process is ergodic with a
unique stationary distribution $\pi$~\cite{MeynTweedie1993}.

Following the approach outlined at the end of Section~\ref{sec:model}, we write $\pi$
in the mixed form
\begin{equation}
  \pi = f_0(x)\,dx + f_1(x)\,dx + \pi_{0,B}\,\delta_{(0,B)} + \pi_{1,0}\,\delta_{(1,0)},
  \label{eq:ansatz}
\end{equation}
where $f_n \geq 0$ are densities on $(0,B)$ and $\pi_{0,B}, \pi_{1,0} \geq 0$ are point
masses at the corners where the flow accumulates: $x=B$ in mode~$0$ (since $r(0,\cdot)=p_0>0$)
and $x=0$ in mode~$1$ (since $r(1,\cdot)=-c<0$). The other two corners carry no atoms.

\textbf{Steady-state equations.}
Setting $\mathcal{A}^*\pi = 0$ and using \eqref{eq:gen0}--\eqref{eq:gen1}, the
stationarity condition $\int \mathcal{A}\psi\,d\pi = 0$ for all
$\psi \in \mathcal{D}(\mathcal{A})$ yields, after integration by parts, the following
system. The \emph{interior equations} on $(0,B)$ are
\begin{align}
  p_0\, f_0'(x) &= -\lambda\, f_0(x) + \mu p_2\, f_1(x), \label{eq:E0} \\
  -c\, f_1'(x)  &= \phantom{-}\lambda\, f_0(x) - \mu p_2\, f_1(x). \label{eq:E1}
\end{align}
The \emph{boundary flux equations} are obtained by flux conservation at each boundary
point: mass arriving at a boundary via the flow must equal mass leaving via jumps.
At $x=0$ in mode $1$, the inward flow $c\,f_1(0^+)$ feeds the atom $\pi_{1,0}$, which
drains via departures at rate $\mu p_1$:
\begin{equation}
  c\,f_1(0^+) = \mu p_1\,\pi_{1,0}. \label{eq:B1}
\end{equation}
At $x=0$ in mode $0$, the atom $\pi_{1,0}$ is created by departures and immediately
carried into the interior by the flow at rate $p_0$:
\begin{equation}
  p_0\,f_0(0^+) = \mu p_1\,\pi_{1,0}. \label{eq:B0}
\end{equation}
At $x=B$ in mode $0$, the inward flow $p_0\,f_0(B^-)$ feeds the atom $\pi_{0,B}$,
which drains via arrivals at rate $\lambda$:
\begin{equation}
  p_0\,f_0(B^-) = \lambda\,\pi_{0,B}. \label{eq:B2}
\end{equation}
At $x=B$ in mode $1$, arrivals from $\pi_{0,B}$ feed the interior flow at rate $\lambda$:
\begin{equation}
  c\,f_1(B^-) = \lambda\,\pi_{0,B}. \label{eq:B3}
\end{equation}
These are supplemented by normalization:
$\int_0^B (f_0 + f_1)\,dx + \pi_{0,B} + \pi_{1,0} = 1$.

We can now state the main result of this section.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:stationary}
Let $\theta = \mu p_2/c - \lambda/p_0$. The unique stationary distribution of $Z(t)$
has the form~\eqref{eq:ansatz} with
\begin{equation}
  f_0(x) = \frac{e^{\theta x}}{K}, \qquad
  f_1(x) = \frac{p_0}{c}\cdot\frac{e^{\theta x}}{K}, \qquad x \in (0,B),
  \label{eq:densities}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \pi_{1,0} = \frac{p_0}{\mu p_1\, K}, \qquad
  \pi_{0,B} = \frac{p_0\,e^{\theta B}}{\lambda\, K},
  \label{eq:atoms}
\end{equation}
where the normalization constant is
\begin{equation}
  K = \frac{p_0+c}{c}\cdot\frac{e^{\theta B}-1}{\theta}
    + \frac{p_0}{\mu p_1} + \frac{p_0\,e^{\theta B}}{\lambda},
  \label{eq:K}
\end{equation}
with the convention $(e^{\theta B}-1)/\theta = B$ when $\theta = 0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[sketch]
\textbf{Decoupling.} Adding \eqref{eq:E0} and \eqref{eq:E1} gives
$\tfrac{d}{dx}[p_0 f_0 - c f_1] = 0$, so $p_0 f_0 - c f_1$ is constant on $(0,B)$.
Equations \eqref{eq:B1} and \eqref{eq:B0} together give $p_0 f_0(0^+) = c f_1(0^+)$,
so the constant is zero: $f_1(x) = (p_0/c)\,f_0(x)$ for all $x \in (0,B)$.

\textbf{Exponential solution.} Substituting $f_1 = (p_0/c) f_0$ into \eqref{eq:E0}
yields $f_0'(x) = \theta\, f_0(x)$ with $\theta = \mu p_2/c - \lambda/p_0$, whose
solution is $f_0(x) = f_0(0^+)\,e^{\theta x}$.

\textbf{Atoms and normalization.} The atoms follow directly from \eqref{eq:B0}
and \eqref{eq:B2}. One can verify that \eqref{eq:B3} is automatically satisfied,
confirming consistency. Setting $f_0(0^+) = 1/K$ and imposing normalization
determines $K$ as in \eqref{eq:K}.

\textbf{Boundary conditions at $x=B$.} Equations \eqref{eq:B2}--\eqref{eq:B3}
are both satisfied by the exponential solution with the atoms given in \eqref{eq:atoms},
confirming that no additional constraint on $\theta$ arises: the parameter $\theta$ is
determined entirely by the interior ODE, not by the boundary.
\end{proof}



The parameter $\theta = \mu p_2/c - \lambda/p_0$ has a precise physical interpretation.
Consider a single idle-busy cycle in the absence of battery boundaries: the battery
charges at rate $p_0$ during the idle period (mean duration $1/\lambda$) and discharges
at rate $c$ during the busy period (mean duration $1/(\mu p_2)$). The expected net
battery change per cycle is
\[
  \Delta = \frac{p_0}{\lambda} - \frac{c}{\mu p_2},
\]
One verifies directly that $\theta > 0$ if and only if $\Delta > 0$: both are
equivalent to $\lambda/\mu < p_0 p_2/c$. Thus $\theta$ is positive when the offered
load $\lambda/\mu$ is below the threshold $p_0 p_2/c$, meaning the battery gains energy
on average per cycle, and negative when it loses energy. Thus $\theta > 0$ corresponds to a lightly loaded station where the battery
tends to fill, mass concentrates near $x = B$, and blocking is close to the M/M/1/1
value at the fast rate $p_2$. When $\theta < 0$ the battery tends to drain on average,
mass concentrates near $x = 0$, and a significant fraction of service occurs at the slow
rate $p_1$. When $\theta = 0$ the expected gain per cycle is exactly zero and the
stationary density is uniform on $(0, B)$.

We now derive the key performance metrics directly from Theorem~\ref{thm:stationary}.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:blocking}
The stationary blocking probability of the system is
\begin{equation}
  P_B = \int_0^B f_1(x)\,dx + \pi_{1,0}
      = \frac{p_0}{K}\left(\frac{e^{\theta B}-1}{c\,\theta} + \frac{1}{\mu p_1}\right).
  \label{eq:PB}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
This follows directly by the PASTA property of arrivals. Note that this probability is also the probability that the charger is working. As $B \to 0$, $P_B \to \lambda/(\lambda + \mu p_1)$, the blocking probability of an $M/M/1/1$ queue at rate $p_1$. As $B \to \infty$ with $\theta > 0$,
$P_B \to \lambda/(\lambda + \mu p_2)$, corresponding to service always at the fast rate $p_2$. When $\theta < 0$, $P_B$ converges to a value strictly between these two limits even as $B \to \infty$, reflecting the residual probability of finding the battery depleted.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:meantime}
The mean service time of an admitted car is:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}[T] = \frac{P_B}{\lambda(1-P_B)}.
  \label{eq:ET}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
This follows directly from Little's law and the fact that only one car is present with probability $P_B$.

\begin{proposition}[Slowdown factor]
\label{prop:slowdown}
Given admission at level $x$ with demand $Y=y$, let
$T(x,y) = y/p_2 + (p_1^{-1}{-}p_2^{-1})\max(y - p_2 x/c, 0)$ be the charging time.
The \emph{slowdown factor} $S(y) = \mathbb{E}[T \mid Y=y]/(y/p_2) \geq 1$
quantifies degradation relative to ideal fast charging.
With $\tilde{x}(y) = cy/p_2$ the critical battery level:

\noindent For $y < p_2 B/c$ (battery may survive):
\begin{equation}
  S(y) = 1 + \frac{p_2-p_1}{p_1\,y\,K(1-P_B)\,\theta}
         \left[\frac{p_2}{c\,\theta}\!\left(e^{\theta cy/p_2}-1\right) - y\right].
  \label{eq:Sy1}
\end{equation}
For $y \geq p_2 B/c$ (battery always depletes):
\begin{multline}
  S(y) = 1 + \frac{p_2-p_1}{p_1\,y\,K(1-P_B)}
         \Bigl\{\tfrac{1}{\theta}\bigl[(y-\tfrac{p_2 B}{c})e^{\theta B} - y
         + \tfrac{p_2(e^{\theta B}-1)}{c\theta}\bigr] \\
         + \tfrac{p_0 e^{\theta B}}{\lambda}(y-\tfrac{p_2 B}{c})\Bigr\}.
  \label{eq:Sy2}
\end{multline}
$S(y)$ satisfies $S(y) \geq 1$, is increasing in $y$, tends to $1$ as $y \to 0$
or $B \to \infty$, and tends to $p_2/p_1$ as $y \to \infty$ or $B \to 0$.
\end{proposition}

\section{The Many-Server Case}
\label{sec:multiserver}
% -------------------------------------------------------

We now extend the model to $s \geq 1$ parallel chargers sharing a single battery buffer
of capacity $B$ and a single grid connection of rate $p_1$. The station continues to
operate as a loss system: a car that arrives when all $s$ chargers are busy is turned away.

\textbf{Assumptions.} We impose $p_1 < s p_2$, so the grid alone cannot sustain all $s$
chargers simultaneously at full rate; the battery is essential at full occupancy. We
also set $p_0 = p_1$, so the idle recharge rate equals the grid supply rate;
this simplifies the structure of the vector field.

\textbf{State space and dynamics.} The state is $Z(t)=(n(t),x(t))$ with
$n(t)\in\{0,\ldots,s\}$ busy chargers and $x(t)\in[0,B]$;
$E=\{0,\ldots,s\}\times[0,B]$ is compact. With $n$ busy chargers each drawing
$p_2$ and the grid supplying $p_1$, the net drift is $r_n = p_1-np_2$.
Setting $p_0=p_1$ makes this hold for all $n$: the battery charges at rate
$p_1$ when idle and drains at rate $np_2-p_1$ when $n>p_1/p_2$. The vector field is
\begin{equation}
  \dot{x} = \begin{cases}
    r_n & 0 < x < B,\ r_n \neq 0, \\
    0   & \text{otherwise (boundary reflection).}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:vf_multi}
\end{equation}
Define the \emph{critical occupancy} $n^* = \lfloor p_1/p_2 \rfloor \in \{0,\ldots,s-1\}$;
the battery charges when $n \leq n^*$ and drains when $n > n^*$.

The jumps are: arrivals at rate $\lambda$ when $n < s$, transitioning $n \to n+1$ with
$x$ unchanged; departures at total rate $n\mu p_2$ when $x > 0$, and at rate $\mu p_1$
when $x = 0$ (the grid supplies $p_1$ shared equally, giving total throughput $\mu p_1$
regardless of occupancy), each transitioning $n \to n-1$ with $x$ unchanged.

The process $Z(t)$ is again a PDMP on a compact state space, and the same
martingale-problem argument as in Section~\ref{sec:model} establishes the strong Markov
property. Irreducibility follows by the same reachability argument, so $Z(t)$ is ergodic
with a unique stationary distribution.

\textbf{Form of the stationary distribution.} The flow direction at each boundary point
now depends on the occupancy level $n$. Atoms accumulate at $(n, B)$ for $n \leq n^*$
(where the flow pushes toward $x = B$) and at $(n, 0)$ for $n > n^*$ (where the flow
pushes toward $x = 0$). We therefore seek a stationary distribution of the form
\begin{equation}
  \pi = \sum_{n=0}^{s} f_n(x)\,dx
      + \sum_{n=0}^{n^*} \pi_n^{(B)}\,\delta_{(n,B)}
      + \sum_{n=n^*+1}^{s} \pi_n^{(0)}\,\delta_{(n,0)},
  \label{eq:form_multi}
\end{equation}
where $f_n \geq 0$ are densities on $(0,B)$ and $\pi_n^{(B)}, \pi_n^{(0)} \geq 0$ are
point masses.

\textbf{Interior equations.} Setting $\mathcal{A}^*\pi = 0$ on $(0,B)$ gives, for
$0 \leq n \leq s$,
\begin{multline}
  r_n f_n'(x) = \lambda f_{n-1}(x)\,\mathbf{1}_{n>0}
              + (n{+}1)\mu p_2\, f_{n+1}(x)\,\mathbf{1}_{n<s} \\
              - (\lambda\,\mathbf{1}_{n<s} + n\mu p_2)\, f_n(x).
  \label{eq:En_multi}
\end{multline}

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:multi}
Let $\rho = \lambda/(\mu p_2)$. There exists a unique $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$, determined
by the boundary flux equations together with normalization, such that the stationary
densities take the form
\begin{equation}
  f_n(x) = C\,a_n(\theta)\,e^{\theta x}, \qquad x \in (0,B),\ n = 0,\ldots,s,
  \label{eq:fn_multi}
\end{equation}
where $C > 0$ is a normalization constant and the coefficients $a_n(\theta)$ satisfy
the recurrence $a_0 = 1$ and
\begin{equation}
  a_{n+1} = \frac{\theta\,S_n + \lambda\,a_n}{(n+1)\mu p_2},
  \qquad S_n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} r_k\,a_k, \quad n = 0,\ldots,s-1.
  \label{eq:recurrence}
\end{equation}
When $\theta = 0$, the recurrence yields $a_n = \rho^n/n!$ and the distribution reduces
to the classical Erlang-B form with uniform density in $x$. For $\theta \neq 0$, the
blocking probability
\begin{equation}
  P_B = C\,a_s\,\frac{e^{\theta B}-1}{\theta} + \pi_s^{(0)}\,\mathbf{1}_{n^* < s}
  \label{eq:PB_multi}
\end{equation}
deviates from the Erlang-B value, with the battery shifting mass toward $x = B$ when
$\theta > 0$ and toward $x = 0$ when $\theta < 0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[sketch]
Summing~\eqref{eq:En_multi} from $k=0$ to $n$ telescopes the right side to
$-\lambda f_n + (n+1)\mu p_2 f_{n+1}$. The exponential form $f_n = Ca_n e^{\theta x}$
then yields~\eqref{eq:recurrence}. The value of $\theta$ is fixed by the boundary flux
equations together with normalization. For $s=1$ and $p_0=p_1$, one recovers
$\theta = \mu p_2/c - \lambda/p_1$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:stationary}.
\end{proof}

The parameter $\theta$ admits the same physical interpretation as in the single-server
case. In the unconstrained system (infinite battery), the occupancy evolves as an
$M/M/s/s$ queue with Erlang-B stationary weights $\pi_n \propto \rho^n/n!$, giving a
mean battery drift $\bar{r} = p_1 - (\lambda/\mu)(1 - P_B^{\text{Erlang}})$. The sign
of $\bar{r}$ determines the sign of $\theta$: positive when the grid can sustain the
average load (battery fills in the long run) and negative otherwise. The case $\theta = 0$
corresponds exactly to $\bar{r} = 0$, at which point the Erlang-B distribution is recovered.

\textbf{Performance metrics.} The blocking probability is given by~\eqref{eq:PB_multi}.
By PASTA and Little's law the mean charging time follows from $P_B$ exactly as in
Propositions~\ref{prop:blocking} and~\ref{prop:meantime}. The slowdown factor
$S(y)$ generalizes directly: the admitted battery level has the same exponential density
$f_0(x)/({1-P_B})$ on $(0,B)$ (now with $s$-server normalization), and the conditional
charging time $T(x,y)$ is unchanged, so the formulas of
Proposition~\ref{prop:slowdown} apply with the updated $P_B$ and $K = 1/C$.

% -------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
% -------------------------------------------------------

We have studied a battery-buffered EV fast charging station using the framework of
piecewise-deterministic Markov processes. For the single-server loss system we derived
the stationary distribution in closed form, establishing a decoupling property that
separates the battery level from the charger state in steady state, and obtained explicit
expressions for the blocking probability, mean charging time, and slowdown factor. We
extended the analysis to a loss system with $s$ parallel chargers sharing a common
battery, showing that the stationary densities retain an exponential form in the battery
level with occupancy-dependent weights governed by a simple recurrence. In both cases the
key parameter $\theta$, the sign of the mean battery drift, determines whether the system
operates in a battery-rich or battery-scarce regime and controls the deviation of the
blocking probability from the classical Erlang-B value.

The analysis highlights a structural difference from standard fluid queues: the
state-dependent service rate at $x = 0$ couples the discrete and continuous components
in a way that prevents a product-form solution in the multi-server case while still
admitting an exponential form in $x$. Numerical illustrations of the performance metrics
and a study of the optimal battery sizing problem are left for future work.
